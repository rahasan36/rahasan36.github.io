My work follows one line of inquiry: how urban expansion and road networks reshape landscapes, and what that change means for the species and people who share them.

Methodologically, it combines land change modeling, machine learning, statistical modeling, and large-scale spatial analysis. I prefer models that take spatial structure seriously, report uncertainty honestly, and stay interpretable to people other than the modeler.

Recent collaborators include Burak Güneralp, İnci Güneralp, and Lee A. Fitzgerald at Texas A&M, M Mahbub Hossain at the University of Houston, and the Oak Ridge National Laboratory Human Geography Group. I co-convene the AGU Urban Area and Global Change session in 2026.

Building the tools at Esri

Product Engineer II · Redlands, California · 2024 – present

I first heard of Esri in 2011, as a first-year student in Bangladesh, when GIS meant ArcMap. Eleven years later, while driving to Houston with my wife, I received an email inviting me to interview for a summer internship. The offer came the following week.

That summer I found that I enjoyed building as much as researching: how a capability is conceived, how a parameter gets its name, and how an idea becomes something documented, tested, and handed to a stranger.

I returned full time in January 2024 and work in geospatial research and development on spatial and raster analysis. What I help design reaches a large user base in government, utilities, conservation, industry, and research.

That reach raises the bar for a method. It has to hold up on data and questions its designer never saw, and the same standard now shapes how I build and validate the models in my own research.

The doctoral years at Texas A&M

PhD in Geography · Texas A&M University · 2019 – 2024

I completed my PhD under Burak Güneralp. The dissertation produced one of the first global-scale road network growth models, calibrated against historical road expansion and projected under several development scenarios. AGU's Eos magazine covered the work.

What I valued most was the range. The projects ran from the thermal environment of a single city to the entire terrestrial protected area network.

The methods had to stretch to match: hazard models that carry their own uncertainty, machine learning for land cover, spatial statistics for global patterns, and process models of how cities and transport networks grow together.

Working at the global scale taught me which questions survive the jump from a case study and which fall apart. I also taught GIS at the undergraduate and graduate levels and mentored fourteen students in spatial analysis and habitat change research.

College Station also gave me something a transcript does not record. Howdy took a while to stop sounding like a performance and start sounding like what it is: a greeting an entire campus means.

I learned the traditions from the inside, from the Ring to Muster, and I did not expect to be claimed by a place so completely. I am an Aggie for life.

Two degrees in Iowa

MCRP, MSc, Graduate Certificate in GIS · Iowa State University · 2017 – 2019

Before Texas came Ames: a master's in Community and Regional Planning, a second in Transportation, and a graduate certificate in GIS.

I taught GIS labs in Community and Regional Planning and led the move from ArcMap to ArcGIS Pro across the undergraduate and graduate courses. It was a practical lesson in how software is adopted.

As a GIS technician at the Iowa State GIS Facility, I built and validated spatial datasets for the Agricultural Conservation Planning Framework across Iowa, Indiana, and Ohio, and reported findings to USDA and Iowa DNR.

That job taught me more about data preparation than any course did. My habit of checking one dataset against another dates from there.

Where it started: Khulna

BURP and MURP · Khulna University, Bangladesh

My undergraduate and master's degrees in Urban and Rural Planning are from Khulna University. I was the first student in the discipline to earn a perfect 4.00 in a semester, and I graduated first in my class with a 3.89 CGPA.

Planning in Bangladesh is not abstract. Cities grow faster than the infrastructure that serves them, in a delta that floods. Accessibility, transport equity, and hazard exposure were daily conditions, not research topics.

My earliest papers, on public transport performance and metro rail accessibility in Dhaka, began the thread that runs through my work today.

Graduate study in the United States had been the plan for years, and I left to pursue it. The planning identity came with me: I remain more interested in what a model implies for a decision than in the model itself.

Away from the screen

Travel, photography, and football

I am Bangladeshi, married, and live in the United States. My wife and I travel whenever we can, mostly to national parks: Glacier, Yellowstone, Grand Teton, the Colorado Plateau, and Big Bend.

Visiting every national park before I turn forty is a real goal. There are photographs from some of the trips.

Photography is the other half of that habit. A camera makes you notice light, structure, and edges, which is not far from reading a land cover raster, only slower and in better weather.

I follow football closely. It is the one subject where I hold strong opinions I cannot support with data.
